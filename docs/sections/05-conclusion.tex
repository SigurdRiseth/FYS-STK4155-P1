\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We fitted polynomials of degree up to 20 to noisy samples of Runge's function
with OLS, Ridge and Lasso, selected models with the bootstrap and $k$-fold
cross-validation, and solved the regression problems with our own gradient-descent
code. The central finding is that the resampling methods, not
the regression methods, limit model selection here. With $n = 100$ random
inputs, the true test error of OLS is flat between $p\approx10$ and $20$, but
the bootstrap and CV estimates grow by orders of magnitude at high degree,
because every resampled fit must extrapolate to held-out points outside its
training range, and a high-degree polynomial diverges there, a statistical
echo of Runge's phenomenon. The estimators therefore select too low a degree
(median 8 for the bootstrap, 10--12 for CV, against 14 for the true optimum),
the more so the fewer distinct points each fit uses. Ridge suppresses the
directions of small singular values that cause these failures, which lets CV
choose higher degrees with the same variance, but the selected penalties are
tiny and the gain is marginal: the CV error drops by a median of $5\%$, the
held-out error not at all, and the true error by $0.2\%$. Lasso, fitted by
subgradient descent, did worse than OLS, because our solver does not reach the
Lasso solution at high degree. On the optimization side, the condition number of the
Hessian, which grows exponentially with the degree, explains all our results:
plain GD needs about $\kappa$ iterations, momentum and Adam reach the
closed-form solution 25 and 9 times faster at $p = 5$, no first-order method
reaches the OLS solution at $p\geq10$, a Ridge penalty of $10^{-3}$ restores
convergence, and subgradient descent for Lasso levels off at a distance from the
optimum set by its step size and never produced an exact zero.

Our study has clear limitations. The true error, which we used to judge the
estimators, requires the known $f$ and is unavailable in practice; the same
holds for the bias against $f$. We studied a single noise level and sample
size for the model selection, a uniform random design, and one set of folds per
data set, so the size of the extrapolation effect is specific to these
choices; on an equispaced or Chebyshev grid, which contain the endpoints of the
interval, it would be much smaller. The bootstrap estimates of bias and
variance are means over replicates and are dominated by the few replicates that
extrapolate badly; a robust estimate (median) would separate the two effects.
The optimizer comparison used a learning-rate grid with four values per decade
and fixed momentum and Adam parameters, which is too coarse to rank momentum
and Adam where they differ by less than a factor of two, and it measures
iterations, not time. Our Lasso results are limited by the solver: with a fixed
number of 5000 Adam steps, the high-degree Lasso fits in the cross-validation
are far from converged, so the Lasso model selection and comparison measure our
optimizer as much as the Lasso model itself. Finally,
all comparisons on the held-out data rest on 20 test points, whose MSE has a
standard error of about $30\%$ of $\sigma^2$.

Two extensions follow directly. First, replacing the monomials $x^j$ by
orthogonal polynomials (Legendre or Chebyshev) on $[-1,1]$ spans the same model
space but keeps $\kappa(\bm{X})$ small; gradient descent, including the
subgradient method for Lasso, would then converge at every degree, and the
coefficients would become interpretable. Second, we would make the
resampling estimates less sensitive to extrapolation, for instance by assigning
the smallest and largest $x$ to the training part of every fold, or by
aggregating fold errors with the median, and test whether the selected degree
then approaches the true optimum. Both are needed before the conclusions of this
study can be transferred to more advanced techniques such as neural networks,
whose optimization is equally sensitive to conditioning.
